Privacy policy for Night Courier

Effective: 21 November 2026

This is an English translation. The German version is the binding one; where the two differ, the German version prevails.

In short

  • Night Courier is a private app for letters between friends. I run it on my own, as a private individual in Switzerland – no company, and no money made from it.
  • So the Swiss Federal Act on Data Protection (FADP) applies to me. If you live in the EU or the EEA, the GDPR also applies to your data (section 1).
  • There are no ads, no tracking, no analytics tools, no push notifications and nothing to buy. I do not sell data.
  • I store only what the app needs to work: your email address, your password (only in a form nobody can read), your username and display name, your city, your friendships, your letters, whom you block and what you report. On top of that comes technical data, such as your IP address, from signing in and in logs.
  • The database is kept by Supabase, a US service, on servers in Zurich, Switzerland (sections 7 and 8). This policy and the support page are hosted by GitHub, also a US service.
  • Letters are not end-to-end encrypted. Technically, Supabase and I could read them. I read letters only when they are reported to me, or when I cannot fix a technical fault any other way.
  • You can delete your account in the app at any time, unless I have suspended it (section 3.8). Every letter you sent or received goes with it, including from the friends you wrote with. Some things remain for a while afterwards, above all reports: for up to 180 days (section 11).
  • Night Courier is for people aged 13 and over. Anyone under 16 needs the permission of a parent or guardian.
  • You have rights, such as access and erasure. Just write to me: vinnivinz@live.de.

2. What this is about

Night Courier is an app for slow letters. Everyone has three bats. A bat carries one letter and flies the real distance between your city and your friend's: the further, the longer – at least 20 minutes within the same city, days across a continent.

Letters only travel between people who have both accepted each other as friends. There is no search, no public profile and no way to write to a stranger.

The rules for using the app are in the house rules, in the app ("Your profile" → "House rules").

3. What data I process, and why

3.1 Your account

When you sign up, you enter your email address, a password, a username and a display name.

  • Email address. You sign in with it, and that is where the email with a code goes if you forget your password (section 3.10). I also use it to write to you when I need to – for example a warning after a report, a message when you have reported a threat (section 3.7), or notice of an important change to this policy (section 15). Other users never see your email address.
  • Password. Only a so-called hash (bcrypt) is stored. Nobody can work your password out from it, not even me. The app itself never stores your password.
  • Account details. Your account has a random user ID and some times: when the account was created, when you last signed in, when your email address counts as confirmed and when an email with a code was last sent to it – and, if it comes to that, a ban (section 3.8).
  • A copy of your names from sign-up. Supabase also keeps the username and display name you enter at sign-up in your account record. Your profile is made from it; after that the copy is not needed. A daily job deletes it, at the latest one day after sign-up. Until then it is also in the sign-in token on your device (section 5).

Why: to give you an account and provide the app. Legal basis: Art. 6(1)(b) GDPR – the data is needed to provide the app to you as the house rules describe.

3.2 Your profile and your city

  • Username. How friends find you: anyone who wants to send you a friend request has to know your exact username. It cannot be changed.
  • Display name. The name your friends see, and anyone you send a request to. You can change it in your profile at any time.
  • Your city. You choose it from a list. The app never asks for your location and does not use GPS. From the distance between two cities the server works out how long a bat flies.
  • Moving. Until a letter has gone to or from you, a new city counts at once, as your first one did: there is no flight yet that a move could shorten. After that, a new city only becomes the one your bats fly from after 24 hours – otherwise anyone could "borrow" a friend's city for one short flight. To make that work, I note on your profile whether a letter has ever gone to or from you; the note stays as long as your account, even after the letters themselves are gone. And when you change city after that, I store your previous city and when you changed. Once the 24 hours are over, a daily job deletes both, at the latest two days after the change.
  • Times. I also store when your profile was created and when it last changed. Setting the note about your first letter counts as a change, and so does the daily job's tidying up after a move.

Legal basis: Art. 6(1)(b) GDPR.

3.3 Friendships and requests

I store who sent whom a request, whether it was accepted, when it was made and when it was accepted. An open request stays until it is accepted, declined or withdrawn; it does not expire on its own. When a request is declined or withdrawn, or a friendship is ended or broken off by a block, I delete the record.

You can make at most 20 new friend requests an hour. To keep to that, I store for your account when the hour of your latest requests began and how many new requests you made in it – not whom you asked. A daily job deletes this once that hour began more than a day ago, at the latest two days after it began; it also goes with your account.

Legal basis: Art. 6(1)(b) GDPR; for the count of new requests, Art. 6(1)(f) GDPR – the legitimate interest in keeping anyone from flooding others with friend requests.

3.4 Letters

For each letter I store:

  • the text (1 to 2,000 characters),
  • who sent it, and to whom,
  • which bat carries it,
  • which city it flies from and to – as the cities were when it was sent – and the distance,
  • when it was sent, when it is due to land, and when it landed,
  • when the recipient first opened it. Only the recipient sees this; there are no read receipts,
  • a technical reference, so that a letter is not sent twice.

Letters are stored as text in the database. They are encrypted on the way there (HTTPS), and Supabase encrypts the stored data. But there is no end-to-end encryption. That means: technically, Supabase and I can read every letter. I read letters only when they have been reported to me (section 3.7), or when I cannot fix a technical fault any other way.

You can write whatever you like in a letter – including very personal things, such as your health or your beliefs. I do not analyse letters and use them for nothing but delivering them – unless a letter is reported (section 3.7). Still, think about what you write: whoever gets your letter can keep it, copy it or take a picture of it.

Nobody can delete a single letter, neither the sender nor the recipient, and there is no period after which letters are deleted on their own. A letter stays stored until one of you deletes your account, or I remove one of the two accounts – then it disappears for both of you. Only when a letter has been reported does the report keep a copy of it (section 3.7).

Legal basis: Art. 6(1)(b) GDPR.

3.5 Your bats

For each of your three bats I store whether it is at home or out, and when it last left or came home. That is how the server makes sure everyone has exactly three bats. While one is flying, the app shows you whom it is flying to and when it arrives.

Legal basis: Art. 6(1)(b) GDPR.

3.6 Blocking

When you block someone, I store who blocked whom, and when. Your friendship (or an open request) ends, they can no longer write to you or ask to be friends, and their letters are hidden from you until you unblock them. The letters are not deleted.

The blocked person is not told. They will notice that you are no longer friends, though, and the letters you sent them stay with them. Only you see your list of blocked people ("Blocked couriers").

A block stays until you lift it or one of you deletes your account. Unblocking does not make you friends again.

Legal basis: Art. 6(1)(b) GDPR, because blocking is a feature of the app, and Art. 6(1)(f) GDPR – the legitimate interest in protecting you from messages you do not want.

3.7 Reporting

You can report a letter or a person. A report contains:

  • who reported, and who was reported,
  • the reason (from a list) and, if you like, a note from you (at most 500 characters),
  • a copy of the reported person's username and display name as they were at the time,
  • for a reported letter, a copy of its text and when it was sent,
  • when the report was made, and later my own note on what I did about it.

A report about you therefore also holds details that do not come from you, but from the person who reported you: the reason and their note.

Who sees a report: only me; it is stored at Supabase (section 7). Not even you can look at your report afterwards. The person reported is not told who reported them – unless the law requires me to. But a letter has only one recipient: if I act on a reported letter, its sender may guess who reported it.

When you report, "also block" is already switched on. You can switch it off if you still want to get letters from that person. If you report the same letter again, or the same person again without a letter, before you close the app or sign out, it starts as you left it the last time.

What I do with a report: I read every report. Depending on what happened, I do nothing, send the person a warning to the email address of their account, or remove their account (section 3.8). If it is about a threat, I suspend the reported account at once, but remove it only a week later. Before that, I write to the person who reported it, at the email address of their account: every letter between the two of you goes with the other account, and they should be able to keep copies first. If you blocked the person when you reported them – which happens unless you switch it off – their letters are hidden from you (section 3.6). To make copies, unblock them first: "Your profile" → "Blocked couriers" → "Unblock". You can block them again afterwards, from one of their letters. With threats or possible crimes, I may pass a report on to the police, together with the reported account's sign-in data as far as it is still stored by then: its sessions and its entries in the sign-in log, each with an IP address (section 3.9).

How long: a report is deleted automatically 180 days after it was made – whether it has been dealt with or not. If either person deletes their account before then, the report still stays until then: without the deleted person's user ID, but with the copied names, the copied letter text and the note. Otherwise someone could take back a threat, along with the only proof of it, by deleting their account. If I pass a report on to the police, the police keep their copy under their own rules, beyond the 180 days too; I delete my own exported copy after handing it over.

Legal basis: Art. 6(1)(f) GDPR. My legitimate interest – and that of everyone who uses Night Courier – is preventing and dealing with abuse, protecting people, especially children and young people, and keeping evidence a victim might need. Where I have to hand a report over because the law requires me to, the legal basis is Art. 6(1)(c) GDPR.

3.8 Removed and banned accounts

If someone breaks the house rules seriously or repeatedly, I remove their account. Their profile, bats, friendships, blocks and every letter the account sent or received are then deleted – on both sides. If it is about a threat, I suspend the account at once, but nothing is deleted yet. I remove it a week later, so that the person who reported it can keep copies first (section 3.7).

A suspended account stops working at once: it can no longer write to anyone, change its name or city, read its letters or see its friends, and it cannot be deleted in the app. The app says that the account has been suspended, and offers to sign out or to contact me; if it is your account and you want it deleted, write to me. Signing in to it is answered as if the password were wrong, so that the app does not tell anyone who merely knows the email address what has become of the account: that takes the code from "I forgot my password", which reaches no one but the owner of the mailbox, and the app then says that the account is suspended. Letters it sent that are still in the air land as usual, and its friends can still send it letters: refusing their letters would tell them of the suspension.

So that the same person cannot simply sign up again with the same email address, I ban the account's sign-in when I remove it; signing in with that address is then answered as for a suspended account. The account record then stays at Supabase: the email address, the password hash and the times from section 3.1. It is kept for two years from the removal; then a daily job deletes it. Its sessions end sooner: a daily job deletes each once it has gone unused for 90 days, and a banned account can no longer use them. Its entries in the sign-in log are deleted once they are 30 days old (section 3.9).

This ban is only for accounts removed for breaking the rules. An account deleted for any other reason – by its owner, by me at its owner's request, or by me in the cases in sections 3.10 and 10 – leaves no ban behind: its email address can be used again at once.

Legal basis: Art. 6(1)(f) GDPR – the legitimate interest in protecting everyone else from someone who was removed for breaking the rules.

3.9 Sign-in, sessions and logs

Keeping you signed in and the app safe creates technical data at Supabase. Every request the app makes to the server carries your IP address and a technical label for your device or operating system (the so-called user agent).

  • Sessions. Each sign-in creates a session with your IP address, user agent and times. When you sign out ("Sign out"), the app asks the server to delete your sessions, for all your devices, and signs you out on this device within a few seconds, whether or not the server has answered by then. Another device that is still signed in keeps working until its sign-in would next be renewed – for up to an hour – and then has to sign in again. Sessions are also deleted with your account. A session that has not been used for 90 days – on a device where you have not opened the app for that long – is deleted by a daily job; there you sign in again. If the server does not hear of your sign-out – without a network, for example – your sessions there stay until that job deletes them; this device's only until you next sign in on it, if you have not closed the app in between, since the app then tells the server. To end them all sooner, sign in and out once more. If you lose a phone, sign in and out on another device – the sign-in on the lost phone then stops working within the hour – or write to me.
  • Sign-in tokens. While the app is open, your sign-in is renewed about once an hour. Each renewal creates a new token, and the old one stops working. Tokens are kept at most as long as the session they belong to.
  • Sign-in log. Supabase logs events such as sign-up, sign-in, sign-out, every renewal of your sign-in, requesting a new password and changing it, each with your user ID, email address, time and, where recorded, IP address. It shows when you used the app. A daily job deletes every entry after 30 days. When you delete your account, or I delete it – at your request, or in the cases in sections 3.10 and 10 – your entries are deleted with it at once. Only an account I remove for breaking the rules keeps its entries until they are 30 days old (section 3.8).
  • Server logs. Supabase logs requests to the server: IP address, time, the address requested (which can contain your user ID and, when you change your city, the ID of the city your bats currently fly from, or the information that no letter has gone to or from you yet), user agent and result, for sign-ins also your email address, and database error messages. The app sends letters, notes and names in the body of a request, not in its address, so they do not normally end up in these logs. A database error message can contain a single value, though, such as a username that is already taken at sign-up. Supabase deletes these logs automatically after a short time.

Legal basis: Art. 6(1)(b) GDPR as far as your sign-in is concerned. Otherwise Art. 6(1)(f) GDPR: my legitimate interest is running the app safely and reliably, spotting attacks and abuse, and finding faults.

3.10 Emails from Night Courier

The only emails Night Courier sends you automatically are about your account: a code to set a new password when you ask for one, and – once it is switched on – a code to confirm your email address at sign-up. You type the code into the app. It has 8 digits, works once and for a day, and only the newest one counts. The emails contain no link. No newsletters, no ads. When I write to you myself (section 3.1), the email comes from my mailbox (section 3.11).

At the moment, an email address is not yet confirmed at sign-up. If someone has used your email address for an account without your knowledge, write to me, and I will delete that account. Once confirmation is switched on, a daily job deletes every sign-up whose email address is still not confirmed 7 days after the last code sent to confirm it, profile and all. If you sign up with your address yourself before then, the code that confirms it makes that account yours: confirming the address deletes the password set before, and the app gives the account the password and the display name you chose – or, if the account has another username, deletes it and sets up yours in its place, with a new code. If you use "I forgot my password" for such an account instead, its code deletes the account, and you can then sign up. If the answer to your code is lost on the way – the connection breaks just then – your address can be confirmed without the app having given the account your password and names. You then set a password with "I forgot my password", and the account keeps the username and display name it had: if they are not the ones you chose, delete the account in your profile and sign up again, as the app tells you.

Legal basis: Art. 6(1)(b) GDPR.

3.11 When you write to me

When you write to me – through "Contact" in the app or to vinnivinz@live.de, with a question, something to add to a report, or a request about your data – I process your email address, your name if it is in the email, and what you write. My mailbox is with Microsoft (Outlook.com). I keep your message as long as I need it for your request, and delete it afterwards.

Legal basis: Art. 6(1)(b) GDPR when it is about your account; Art. 6(1)(c) GDPR when you exercise your data protection rights, because I am then legally required to answer; otherwise Art. 6(1)(f) GDPR – my legitimate interest in answering questions and dealing with reports.

  • Your device's location, the address book on your phone, photos, camera or microphone. The app does not ask for access to any of them. (The city you choose from a list is described in section 3.2, and your friends, requests and blocks in the app in sections 3.3 and 3.6. In the privacy labels Apple and Google show in their stores, these appear under "Location" and "Contacts".)

4. What others can see

  • Your email address – no other user; only you, me as the operator, and Supabase as my processor.
  • When you send someone a request, they see your username and your display name. You see only their username until they accept.
  • Your friends see your username, your display name, your city with its country, and how long a bat takes to reach you.
  • Anyone you have exchanged letters with sees, on each letter, your username, your current display name, and the cities it flew from and to – even after you are no longer friends, for as long as the letter exists. If you change your display name, old letters show the new one too.
  • Anyone who sends you a letter sees when it landed, but never whether or when you opened it.
  • Anyone who has blocked you sees you by your username only, not your display name.
  • Anyone who tries your username can find out that it exists – by sending a friend request to it, or by trying to sign up with it. Usernames can be guessed: a request to a name nobody has is refused, but not counted. One account can make at most 20 new requests an hour (section 3.3), and each request shows you the sender's username and display name. They learn nothing more from that until you accept.
  • Anyone who knows your email address can find out whether it has a Night Courier account. While email addresses are not yet confirmed at sign-up (section 3.10), trying to sign up with it says so; after that, the answers of the sign-in server can still give it away. They learn nothing else about your account from that.
  • Other users do not see whom you have blocked or reported (although that person may guess, sections 3.6 and 3.7), whether you are online, or whether you are writing.

Your random user ID also reaches the app of everyone who is your friend, has sent you a request or received one from you, has exchanged letters with you, or has blocked you; the app does not show it.

5. What is stored on your device

  • On your device the app stores your sign-in (the "session"). It contains an access token with your user ID and your email address, a token for renewing the sign-in, and some details of your account. Until the copy of your names from sign-up has been deleted (section 3.1) and your sign-in has been renewed once after that, those names are in it too.
  • On an iPhone it is in the iOS keychain, readable only while the iPhone is unlocked, and only on this device: it does not move to a new iPhone, not even with a backup; you sign in again there. It may stay in the keychain after the app is deleted – sign out first if you want to be sure.
  • On Android it is encrypted with the Android Keystore. It is excluded from backups and from transfer to a new device.
  • While you are using a code from an email (section 3.10), the app also keeps a note beside the session of what the code is for: your email address and, when you are signing up, the username and display name you typed – never your password. If the app is closed after the code has signed you in but before that is done, it carries on the next time it opens. The note is kept where the session is, and deleted once your account is set up or your password saved, when you sign out or sign in with your password, and when the app opens without a sign-in for that address.
  • When you sign out, the app deletes the session and that note from your device, even if the server cannot be reached at that moment. After you delete your account, it does so as soon as it knows that the deletion went through. Until you close the app, it may still keep the session in its memory – it does not store it – to make sure the sign-out holds: if the server could not be reached, the app tells it at your next sign-in.
  • Apart from that, the app stores nothing on your device. Letters, drafts, friend lists and reports are on the device only while the app is open. A letter you have started is gone when you close the app or sign out.
  • What your device does by itself is outside the app: the app switcher can show a picture of an open letter, for example, the keyboard learns words you type, and a password manager may offer to save your password.

6. Legal bases at a glance

These are the legal bases under the GDPR. The FADP does not require such a basis for every processing (section 1).

  • Art. 6(1)(b) GDPR (contract): everything needed to provide the app to you – account, profile, city, friendships, letters, bats, blocking, sign-in and the emails about your account.
  • Art. 6(1)(f) GDPR (legitimate interests): security (sessions and logs), dealing with abuse (reports, warnings, removing accounts, the hourly limit on friend requests), keeping reports for 180 days, keeping removed accounts banned for two years from the removal, passing a report to the police voluntarily, crash reports, and visits to the web pages on GitHub Pages (section 7). The interests behind them: a safe app, protecting the people who use it – children and young people above all – and evidence for victims.
  • Art. 6(1)(c) GDPR (legal obligation): where the law requires me to hand data over, for example to the police, a public prosecutor or a court, and when I answer your requests about your data protection rights.

10. Children and young people – and their parents

  • Night Courier is for people aged 13 and over. Anyone under 16 needs the permission of a parent or guardian. The house rules say so. I chose 16 because it is the highest age any EU country sets under Art. 8 GDPR for agreeing to an online service on one's own. Swiss law sets no fixed age; what counts there is whether someone has the capacity of judgement.
  • I do not check or store anyone's age. At sign-up, it is only confirmed.
  • The app is built so that strangers cannot write to anyone: letters only travel between people who have both accepted each other as friends. Someone who knows the exact username can send a friend request; it shows only their username and display name, and they can be blocked and reported. There are no public profiles, no ads and no tracking. Sexual content involving anyone under 18, or sent to anyone under 18, is forbidden. Anyone who breaks that rule should expect me to remove their account.
  • What parents can do: read this policy and the house rules with their child; delete the account together with their child ("Your profile" → "Delete my account"); and write to me if they have questions, want to report something, or their child has created an account without their permission. If a child under 16 has an account without permission, I delete it when the parents ask. Before that, I may ask questions to make sure the request really comes from the parents. If I learn that someone is younger than 13, I delete their account – without keeping their email address banned (section 3.8 is only for accounts removed for breaking the rules).
  • Good to know: when an account is deleted, all its letters are gone. Anyone who wants to keep a letter – as evidence, for example – should take a screenshot first.

11. Your rights

Under the GDPR you have these rights:

  • Access (Art. 15): you can find out what data I hold about you, and get a copy.
  • Rectification (Art. 16): you can change your display name and city yourself, in your profile. Your username cannot be changed. For your email address, write to me.
  • Erasure (Art. 17): you delete your account yourself, in the app: "Your profile" → "Delete my account". It happens at once and cannot be undone. It deletes your account, your profile, your bats, friendships and blocks, every letter you sent or received – on the other people's side too – and your entries in the sign-in log. What remains afterwards: reports (up to 180 days, section 3.7), server logs and backups until they are deleted automatically, emails between you and me, and what Apple or Google hold about your test version (section 3.12). A suspended account cannot be deleted in the app; then write to me (section 3.8).
  • Restriction (Art. 18): you can ask me to only store some data for now and not use it otherwise – while it is being settled whether it is correct, for example.
  • Data portability (Art. 20): you can get the data you gave me in a common, machine-readable format. There is no export button in the app; write to me.

Under the Swiss FADP you have similar rights: access (Art. 25 FADP – free of charge, and normally within 30 days), getting your data handed over or transferred (Art. 28 FADP) and rectification (Art. 32(1) FADP). You can also demand, in court if need be, that a particular processing stops, that I do not pass your data on to third parties, or that I delete it (Art. 32(2) FADP).

Complaint. The authority responsible for me is the Federal Data Protection and Information Commissioner (FDPIC), Feldeggweg 1, 3003 Bern, Switzerland, www.edoeb.admin.ch. You can turn to it if you think I am breaking the FADP. If you live in the EU or the EEA, you can also complain to a data protection supervisory authority (Art. 77 GDPR), in particular in the country where you live or work, or where the infringement took place. In Germany, that is the data protection authority of your federal state. I would rather you wrote to me first, though.

Right to object (Art. 21): where I process data on the basis of legitimate interests (Art. 6(1)(f) GDPR), you can object at any time, on grounds relating to your particular situation. I then stop processing that data – unless I can show compelling legitimate grounds that override your interests, or the processing is for the establishment, exercise or defence of legal claims. That may be the case with a report about a threat, for example.

How: write to vinnivinz@live.de, ideally from the email address you use for Night Courier – so I can be sure the request comes from you. Otherwise I will ask. I answer within 30 days. It costs nothing.

12. No automated decisions

There are no automated decisions and no profiling within the meaning of Art. 22 GDPR. The server does work out automatically how long a bat flies, but that is not a decision about you. Reports, warnings and removing accounts are decided by me, a human.

13. Security

  • Every connection between the app and the server is encrypted (HTTPS); the app does not even open an unencrypted connection to the server.
  • Supabase encrypts the stored data (AES-256).
  • Passwords are stored only as a hash.
  • The rules on who may see what are enforced by the database itself, not just by the app. Everyone can reach only their own data and what section 4 describes.
  • The app contains only a public key, which opens no personal data without signing in; it never contains the administrative key.
  • Your sign-in is kept in your device's keychain or keystore (section 5).
  • Only I have administrative access to the database (and Supabase, as the technical provider).
  • Letters are not end-to-end encrypted (section 3.4).

You can help: use a password you use nowhere else, and sign out on devices other people use too. Should something still go wrong and data fall into the wrong hands, I will report it to the FDPIC as soon as possible if it is likely to lead to a high risk for the people affected (Art. 24 FADP), and, as far as people in the EU and the EEA are concerned, to the competent supervisory authority, as Art. 33 GDPR requires. Where necessary, I will also tell you (Art. 24(4) FADP, Art. 34 GDPR).
